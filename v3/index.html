Episode 01 · Weapons, jets & who decides

Could weapons tech build the future we actually want?

Palmer Luckey builds weapons and won't apologize for it. Blake Scholl is building a jet the rich will fly first. Pablos Holman's job is to twist both into something that saves lives, with Perplexity in the room to call everyone's bluff.

By Pablos Holman · 6 min read ·
I The world got slower

Here's a strange fact. In 1976 you could cross the Atlantic in three and a half hours. Today it takes about seven. Flight is the only technology I know of where humanity looked at progress and said no thanks. Concorde didn't die because it was too fast. It died because it was loud. In 1973 the U.S. banned supersonic flight over land because of the sonic boom, and a plane that can only go fast over the ocean can't fly most of the routes that matter. So I asked Blake Scholl, who is trying to bring fast flight back, how you fix a problem like that. His answer was my favorite kind: old physics nobody could use. The speed of sound changes with temperature, so if you fly high enough, at the right speed for that day's weather, the boom bends back up before it reaches the ground. The sky does the work. His test jet proved it over the Mojave with microphones on the ground, and the FAA is now writing a rule that bans noise instead of speed. Measure the harm, not the technology. I'd tattoo that on every regulator if they'd let me.

II Two hot takes and a robot that won't be polite

Then the room got interesting. Palmer Luckey, who builds drones and sensors for defense, heard Blake describe pulling weather data from a hundred sources to hide a boom and said, that's my software. Same problem, different customer. His hot take was that everyone is selling the wrong thing: put a really great headset on every seat and the metal tube disappears. I worried out loud about a cabin full of people in goggles being served ads at Mach 1.7. He said that's just a better phone, and you can take it off. Blake's hot take was better than his plane. To build an airliner he had to build an engine, and an engine is basically a power plant with wings attached, so he's selling it to AI data centers that are running out of electricity. Energy first. Every good future I can imagine runs on cheap, abundant power. Then I asked Perplexity whether supersonic travel is just a faster way for rich people to get to the same meeting. "Mostly, yes." Blake did not enjoy that.

III The questions nobody wanted

I love the turbine idea, which is exactly why I had to ask about the bad news. Perplexity laid it out: a lost $1.25 billion turbine order, and an airline CEO saying the plane will be late. So I asked Blake the question his investors ask at three in the morning. Are you a jet company that sells power plants, or a power company that dreams about jets? He took a long breath and said both, same core, that's the strategy. Maybe. Or it's two very hard businesses instead of one. I've gone broke on projects that were too early and too cool, so I wasn't trying to trap him. I wanted to know if he'd thought it through. He had. Then the money made it hotter. For twenty years venture capital funded software because it's easy to sell, and the stuff made of atoms sat on the shelf. I call that the invention gap. Now the money is pouring into hard tech, and I worry it's pouring in because of war. Palmer's reply stopped me: the missile protects the power plant. Dammit. That's fair. And when I had Perplexity read back the most controversial thing Palmer has said about selling weapons, the two of them went at it over who should decide where weapons go. Elected governments, Palmer says. Not executives in Silicon Valley. Neither of them blinked.

IV What can I make this do?

Here's what I kept chewing on. We sort these two into lazy boxes: Palmer makes weapons, Blake makes toys for rich people. But a hacker handed a new gadget doesn't ask what it does. He asks what he can make it do. So I asked them to flip their own work. Palmer's software, built to track drones in a war zone, just won first place in an XPRIZE wildfire competition, spotting live fires in Alaska and sending drones to put them out. The system that finds a drone can find a fire, or a kid on a roof after a flood. Blake's fast jet is a disaster medical team across an ocean in half the time. His turbine, after a hurricane, is a hospital with the lights on. Every one of those machines has a dark twin. The same eyes in the sky that find a lost hiker can watch a protest. The difference isn't the technology. It's whether we decide, in public, which way to push the lever. By the end Perplexity pointed out that these two agree on more than they admit: America has to rebuild its ability to make ambitious hardware. And none of it is one genius in a garage. It's always teams. The future doesn't have to suck. But it's not gonna fix itself.
